# Smart or Stupid: Mad at God?
“How long, O Lord, must I call for help but You do not hear, or cry out to You, “Violence\!” but You do not save?”
‭‭Habakkuk‬ ‭1‬:‭2‬ ‭BSB‬‬

So many of life’s struggles get played out in relationships close to you\. Circumstances don’t turn out, and things are out of your control, yet you are still disappointed\. In our humanity we look for someone or something to blame\. Often, those closest to us get the brunt of our misguided despair\.

The question under this is ominous\. If the circumstances were out of our control, it goes to reason that little or nothing could be don’t differently, apart from foolish second guessing\. The only One that had control to change the outcome was the Lord\. 

***Dare we point our frustration, disappointment, or anger at God?*** 

It is naive to think that a response like this displays a lack of faith\.
1. You cannot be disappointed in someone you do not trust\.
2. This is not a lack of faith; this is faith under strain\.
3. Our disappointment discloses the context of our faith in an authentic and emotional way\.

In Habakkuk’s case, he struggles to reconcile God’s holiness with the prosperity of the wicked and the suffering of the righteous\. He is not abandoning his faith, nor is questioning the character of the Lord\. In his frustration, Habakkuk is seeking understanding, while equally exposing his flaws\. 

Disappointment \(or anger\) have boundaries\. Our response should\.\.\.
•   Be voiced directly to God rather than away from Him
•   Preserve a commitment to God’s character
•   Seek understanding rather than independence
•   Recognize the sovereign, all\-knowing majesty of God

**We must equally be aware of what our frustrations expose about ourself\.** 

Disappointment exposes issues of our heart that would otherwise not be seen\. Selfish motive, lack of patience, or other tainted matters can lie hidden in the dark, and mixed unknowingly into our expectations\. The strain on our faith is the means to purify our heart, casting light on matters we likely would neglect\.

It would be foolish to approach God without the expectation that His holiness will not expose our lack of righteousness\. His holiness always requires a righteous response\.

TITLE: # Gospel of the Kingdom

# Gospel of the Kingdom
**The Kingdom “at hand” and the coming age**
Jesus proclaimed that the Kingdom of God is near/at hand and called people to repent and believe \(Mark 1:15\)\. The Kingdom is already ours in Christ, and we are empowered now to live righteously and witness faithfully—while we await the day Jesus completes the renewal of all things \(Matt\. 6:10; Rom\. 8:18–25\)\.

**Future inheritance**
Our future inheritance is not merely “going to heaven,” but the resurrection of the body, the renewal of creation, and full participation in God’s completed reign—life made permanent and unbroken \(1 Cor\. 15:42–57; Rev\. 21:1–5\)\. The Spirit is given now as the guarantee of what is coming \(Eph\. 1:13–14\)\.

**Living in the Kingdom now**
Because Christ has accomplished the Father’s work \(John 17:4\) and believers are empowered by the Spirit, Christians can live now in a Kingdom manner—through thought, word, and deed—showing mercy, truth, righteousness, peace, and love \(Rom\. 14:17; Matt\. 5:14–16\)\. This is not earning the Kingdom but living as citizens of it \(Phil\. 3:20\)\.

**Eden echoes and “signposts”**
Even in a fallen world, goodness still appears because humans are created in God’s image \(Gen\. 1:26–27\)\. Moments of compassion, mercy, and love can function as Eden echoes—signposts pointing toward what humanity was meant to be and what God’s reign restores\. These moments can occur anywhere, since God shows kindness in the world and sustains human life even among the unjust \(Matt\. 5:45; Acts 14:16–17\)\.

**Can non\-Christians show “Kingdom\-like” signs?**
Yes, non\-Christians can display real virtues \(kindness, sacrifice, justice, compassion\), but Scripture distinguishes between goodness that reflects God’s design and the full reality of the Kingdom, which is tied to new birth, repentance, and faith in the King \(John 3:3–5; Mark 1:15\)\. Virtue alone is not the Kingdom’s fullness, but it may point toward it \(Rom\. 2:14–15\)\.

**The “open door” and readiness**
God is the ultimate pursuer \(John 3:16\)\. The readiness of another person—the “open door”—is ultimately God’s work \(Col\. 4:3\)\. Yet believers are called to walk in wisdom and be ready, speaking with grace and clarity \(Col\. 4:5–6; 1 Pet\. 3:15\)\. An open door is often shown not by a formal invitation, but by receptivity: pain, curiosity, moral honesty, questions, or genuine engagement\.

**Manipulation vs faithful witness**
Scripture condemns deceit, flattery, and coercive tactics \(2 Cor\. 4:2; 1 Thess\. 2:3–5\)\. Christians may wrongly try to control outcomes by manufacturing emotion or forcing responses and calling it “sharing the gospel\.” But the biblical pattern is faithful proclamation with integrity, leaving results to God—because believers plant and water, while God gives the growth \(1 Cor\. 3:6–7\)\.

**The world taking credit for Kingdom\-shaped goods**
People often celebrate justice, mercy, and love as human achievement while crediting Jesus only for “private salvation\.” Biblically, this is incomplete: the Kingdom is not merely personal forgiveness but the reign of God bringing restoration through Christ \(Matt\. 6:10; 1 Cor\. 15:24–26\)\. The Christian task is to affirm real good, live as light, and gently reconnect what is good to its true source and fulfillment in Christ \(Matt\. 5:16\)\.

**Sharing the Gospel**
*Sharing the gospel is connecting a person’s real life—need, guilt, longing, and hope—to Jesus Christ, showing that God is pursuing them and calling them to a change of heart and providing them new life in Christ’s Kingdom\.*

TITLE: # Against the Machine

# Against the Machine
CLIFF NOTES

1. **Executive Overview**

Against the Machine argues that modern Western civilisation has come to be governed by an inhuman force Kingsnorth calls “the Machine\.” This Machine is not merely technology, but a spiritual, cultural, and metaphysical system that prioritises abstraction, control, efficiency, and power over lived human reality\.

Kingsnorth claims that modernity’s faith in Progress, technology, and rational mastery has led not to liberation but to dehumanisation, ecological devastation, and spiritual emptiness\. The crisis of our age is therefore not political or technical, but spiritual\.

The book calls for resistance, refusal, and restoration, grounded in:
•   Christianity \(especially asceticism and humility\),
•   rooted human culture \(place, memory, community\),
•   and limits on power and technological domination\.

⸻

2. ​**Core Thesis**​ •   The modern world is governed by a Machine worldview that treats reality as inert, manipulable, and fungible\. •   This worldview originated in the West and has replaced Christianity with a secular religion of Progress\. •   Technology is not neutral; it reshapes consciousness and moral imagination\. •   Attempts to “fix” the Machine using the Machine’s tools only accelerate its growth\. •   Survival requires withdrawal of consent, not reform\.

⸻

3. **The Machine \(Key Concept\)**

The Machine is:
•   a way of seeing before it is a system of tools,
•   rooted in Enlightenment rationalism and materialism,
•   hostile to limits, mystery, and the sacred\.

Machine values include:
•   efficiency over meaning,
•   scale over intimacy,
•   abstraction over embodiment,
•   information over wisdom,
•   control over communion\.

Technology becomes the Machine’s sacrament — the visible sign of its invisible power\.

⸻

4. **Progress as a False Religion**

Kingsnorth treats Progress as a theology:
•   it promises salvation through growth, innovation, and control,
•   it rejects tradition, limits, and inherited wisdom,
•   it views the past as an obstacle\.

Both capitalism and socialism, left and right, are shown to be Machine ideologies because they share the same assumptions about mastery, efficiency, and technological salvation\.

⸻

5. **Technology Is Not Neutral**

A central claim of the book is the Myth of Neutral Technology\.

Kingsnorth argues:
•   technologies shape the soul and the imagination,
•   digital systems flatten attention, memory, and presence,
•   screens sever us from nature, place, and one another\.

The internet, AI, and digital surveillance are portrayed not just as tools, but as spiritually formative powers that remake what it means to be human\.

⸻

6. **Christianity and the Machine**

Kingsnorth contrasts the Machine with historic Christianity\.

The Machine says:
•   “Do what thou wilt\.”
•   “You will be like gods\.”
•   “Everything can be remade\.”

Christianity says:
•   “Thy will be done\.”
•   “Take up your cross\.”
•   “Accept limits\.”

The Machine reenacts the Fall: choosing knowledge, power, and control over communion with God and creation\.

⸻

7. **Askesis \(Ascetic Resistance\)**

Kingsnorth proposes technological askesis:
•   disciplined refusal of certain technologies,
•   limits on digital immersion,
•   fasting from screens and information\.

Askesis is not about purity or escape, but training the soul to resist domination and regain self\-control\.

⸻

8. **Culture, Place, and Roots**

The book stresses the loss of:
•   place,
•   memory,
•   tradition,
•   and inherited meaning\.

Kingsnorth argues that:
•   globalised, placeless culture serves the Machine,
•   human beings need rootedness to remain human,
•   folk culture, ritual, and story resist abstraction\.

He praises localism, community, and small\-scale life — not as utopian solutions, but as forms of survival\.

⸻

9. **Resistance Without Utopia**

Kingsnorth explicitly rejects:
•   saving the world,
•   political blueprints,
•   technological fixes\.

Instead, he calls for:
•   living truthfully where one is,
•   building parallel cultures,
•   refusal to worship the Machine\.

This resistance may look small, ridiculous, or ineffective — but faithfulness matters more than success\.

⸻

10. **Final Vision**

The book ends with a call to:
•   remain human,
•   remain rooted,
•   remain faithful,
•   despite the Machine’s rise\.

Kingsnorth believes the age of the Machine is not accidental, and that Christians are called to live faithfully within it — not to conquer it\.

⸻

11. **Key Takeaway \(One Sentence\)**

Against the Machine argues that modern technological civilisation is a spiritual rebellion against reality itself, and that only humility, limits, and rooted faith can preserve our humanity\.




# My Summary

The book reads like the story Scripture has been telling all along: people exchange the glory of God for substitutes\. They build a tower of technique \(Ellul\), hoping skill and scale might save them\. And they find themselves lost in a land full of abundance, yet hollowed of meaning\.

The Machine, then, is not the final enemy\. It is the outward shape of an inward turning away\. **An apostasy made visible\.**

Scripture affirms Kingsnorth’s warning that no society survives the loss of its sacred center\. Yet Scripture also presses further than Kingsnorth allows\. God does not abandon His people when they find themselves in exile\. **He meets them there\.**

***The wilderness is not only where idols collapse\. It is where covenant is renewed\. Where dependence is relearned\. Where the voice of God is heard again because there is finally nothing else left to listen to\.***

If Kingsnorth stands as the prophet who deconstructs, Scripture reminds us that God plants and builds\. “To uproot and tear down,” yes, “but also to build and to plant” \(Jeremiah 1:10\)\.

The Bible’s final word is not the domain of the Machine; not hollowing, nor retreat, nor reservation\.

​**The final word is resurrection\.**​ Evil is destroyed, and life is restored from death\.

The question lingering over the book: *Not whether the West can be saved\. But whether, in the ruins of its false gods, the living God might yet be heard again\.*

# “They must be\.\.\.”
‘They must be circumcised,’ they said, ‘and you must tell them to keep the law of Moses\.’
—Acts 15:5

The signs of inclusion\-exclusion riddled the early church much like today\. We could look past this and claim that we are free of circumcision and the Old Testament Law, and might be correct, yet we would miss the underlying point\.

Peter will eventually stand up and say, “Now then, why do you test God by placing on the necks of the disciples a yoke that neither we nor our fathers have been able to bear? On the contrary, ​***we believe it is through the grace of the Lord Jesus that we are saved, just as they are\.***​”
‭‭\(Acts‬ ‭15‬:‭10‬\-‭11\)

Peering through the window on the door of the church, one might ask, “What must I do to be on the other side of the door?”

Perhaps similar to this situation, a teacher of the Scriptures might be quick to answer\. In the early church, the inclusion was the Jews and the exclusion was the Gentiles\. The teacher \(Pharisee\) attempts to answer the question, missing that the premise of the question was wrong, by answering, “Do what we do to be like us\.”

*We miss the premise: “What must I do?”* 

In our day, the outsider asks a similar question\. We rush in to answer, thinking that we are addressing salvation\. We have a long list of ways to explain this answer\. Yet, we miss the core premise: “What must I do to be included \(to get through the door, into the church community\)?”

Is this simply a nuance, or is this a vile corruption of the gospel? Addressing circumcision, we see the corruption, without understanding that what we ask of people might be \(usually is\) equally corrupt\. 

The early church leaders debated and argued over a this question\. This was a critical junction in carrying forth the teachings of Jesus versus the traditions of their upbringing\. “What must I do to be included? To access God like you access God\.”

From a perspective of the mission of God, “It isn’t a matter, it can’t be a matter, of belonging to one particular ethnic group, no matter how sacred, how chosen, how blessed with God’s presence and entrusted with carrying his promise to the world\. It is time for that promise to be delivered, not kept as a private possession\.” \(N\.T\.Wright\)

***It is through the grace of the Lord Jesus that we are saved\.***

Christians today, including me, need to wrestle with what is meant by, “saved through the grace of Jesus\.” We believe, certainly, but our belief is not on the basis of anything we have done\. We believe that salvation comes solely on the basis of what Jesus has done\. The question of “What must I do?” can only be answered by Jesus — He is the giver; we are the recipient\.

Reflect on the conversation Jesus had with the Pharisee Nicodemus:
Q: What must I do? \(To receive the Kingdom of God\.\)
A: Be born again\.
Q: How can that be? \(We only have one birthday\.\)
A: Your second birth is of the Spirit, BUT you do not know where the Spirit comes from or where it is going\. You will not be able to find Him to be born again\.
Q: How can this be? \(What you are saying leaves me with nothing to do with— no path forward— no agency —no control\.\)
A: Jesus explains, not what a person must do, but what He will do, leaving the person empty handed —doing nothing\.

Our explanations of passing through the door are tainted with variations of things we have agency to control\. The words, “You do not have to do anything,” are difficult for us to grasp or explain\. We find value in our involvement, taking some action to achieve the outcome\. We see this even in the subtlety of saying, “I *received* Jesus” as the explanation for salvation\.


###### Personal
In my world, doing nothing is irresponsible\. Do something, even if it is wrong, has value over doing nothing\. These words come to mind, “Have some self respect; do something, anything\.” We must take control or others will control us\.

I can confidently say, this is the greatest struggle of our time\. The belief that doing something \(cause\) makes something happen \(effect\)\. To think this untrue is certain foolishness in our time\. 

The deception is strong, if I do not have it, it is because I have not done the right thing\. Therefore, I must figure out what to change to get the result\.

This deception is heretical to the grace known in the Kingdom of God, and yet it seems foolish in the kingdom of man\. We look for precepts that allow us some level of control \(self\-value\), saying things like:

Pray like it depends on God, and work like it depends on us\.
The Kingdom is here now, but not\-yet\.
A list of what to believe or to do in order to be saved\. \(Or to get someone else saved\.\)
The question of whether a person is saved \(assuming we can know\)\.

**Grace requires Jesus to do something, offering it to us freely, while we do nothing but receive the gift\.**

Without a proper perspective, we are left feeling undervalued, like we are not needed\.

In reality, we are needed by God, yet not in the way the world around us thinks, often including us\. That is a reflection for another day\.

# Absolutely non\-absolute
“Instead, we should write and tell them to abstain from food polluted by idols, from sexual immorality, from the meat of strangled animals, and from blood\.”
‭‭—Acts‬ ‭15‬:‭20‬ ‭BSB‬‬

Is there a place in San Francisco that would deeply offend my Christian colleagues if I participated in the activities it represents?

This is the sentiment of James’ instruction\. Gentiles, new to a faith that, frankly, was new to everyone\. Forgiven of sins, now and forever\. Was that too much freedom? Paul will address this later\. In this moment, the critical issue was grafting together Jew and Gentile into one community lead by Jesus\.

To a Jew, the worship of pagan gods was both offensive and disobedient\. This was a clear line of demarcation\. “You are to have no other gods before Me,” declares the Lord\. The pagan world performed worship rituals that were vile to Jews\. Because of that shouldn’t they also be vile to this new lot of Gentiles called Christians?

Obviously, the leaders of the early church saw the problem that was brewing\. The disclaimer given by James was aimed at unifying the church, not creating new rules \(law\) to live under\.

Questions abound if this is misread:
- What is the authority of the leaders?
- What consequences might fall on those that disregarded the instruction?
- Were there other offenses that needed to be dealt with for the sake of unity?
- What parameters might be needed for various levels of Gentile commitment to Christ \(i\.e\. Seekers\)?

Faced with a similar issue today, one might conclude that the Apostle’s did not go far enough\. They left too much wiggle room\.

Yet, this seems to be the point\. They were concerned that the yoke upon the Jews, which Jesus eliminated, should not now be placed on the Gentiles\. In Peter’s words, “Now then, why do you test God by placing on the necks of the disciples a yoke that neither we nor our fathers have been able to bear?” \(15‬:‭10‬\)

​*“Give a church a rule and you guide them for a day; teach a church to think and you guide them for life\.”*​ \(N\.T\. Wright\)

The intent was to guide the Jews and Gentile into being one body\. This was something unprecedented, and there would be many obstacles in the years to come\.

We can conclude that the early church leaders were not creating a revised set of rules for the church to follow\. If so, Luke might have addressed this differently\. Luke gave us what we needed to see the leaders as shepherds to the church, only exercising authority for the sake of guarding doctrine\.


###### Personal

⸻

Dominant View of Self: Rights\-bearing, self\-governing moral agent
Distinct Shift Phrase: The autonomous citizen emerges

⸻

Dominant View of Self: Self\-made, achievement\-oriented individual

⸻

⸻

Dominant View of Self: Expressive, authenticity\-centered self

⸻
**Late 20th Century \(1980–2005\)**
Dominant View of Self: Consumer\-sovereign chooser
Distinct Shift Phrase: Identity as preference

**Digital Age \(2005–Present\)**
Dominant View of Self: Curated, networked, performative identity

The resurrected Jesus wants that point to be clear\. The image portrayed is “my Father and your Father,” and “my God and your God\.” Jesus expresses this right away — they are no longer students; they are now brothers\.

N\.T\. Wright explains, “Something has altered, decisively\. Something has been achieved\. A new relationship has sprung to life like a sudden spring flower\. The disciples are welcomed into a new world: a world where they can know God the way Jesus knew God, where they can be intimate children with their father\.” \(John for Everyone\)


###### Reflection 
The Easter morning story is familiar, so it is easy to miss important details: the contrast between Lazarus’ tomb and the disciples' hurrying, the contrast between Jesus’ calm and the disciples' hurrying, the key players involved, and the significance of each written element\. The story is a masterpiece\!

We know the ending, but if we didn't, the reader would be left hanging, anticipating how Jesus would explain what happened\.

“See, I told you not to worry\.”
“V for victory\.”
“I am invincible\. Soon to be Lord over all\.”

Your imagination can take this anywhere, yet not many would foresee the intimate words, nor the otherness of the first words\.

Yahweh is my God just as he is to Jesus\. Yahweh is a relationship to be had, not as a ruler, but as a friend\. The magnificent holiness no longer destroys me in its presence; it longs to embrace me\.

The resurrection of Jesus was not a historical narrative; it was the most significant evidence in creation outside creation itself\.

One cannot watch the story\. We are active participants in the story\.

He has risen indeed\! ✝️

TITLE: A Long Week

A Long Week
“Eight days later, His disciples were once again inside with the doors locked, and Thomas was with them\. Jesus came and stood among them and said, “Peace be with you\.”” —John‬ ‭20‬:‭26‬ ‭BSB‬‬

Another Easter detail that I easily read through\. Jesus appears to His disciples, yet Thomas was not there—the excitement, the anticipation, the marvel that Jesus was alive and could appear anywhere\.

Those who first saw Jesus had to have been talking about it\. No, just talking — imagining all that it meant\. And Thomas was not there\!

John tells us that the disciples told Thomas, as if to children talking about Christmas morning\. We don’t know why, but Thomas would not believe them until he saw with his own eyes and touched Jesus’ wounds with his own hand\. 

And there is a small detail in the story: Thomas stayed in a state of uncertainty for eight days\! Jesus would let him ponder all that the others said for eight days\.

###### Reflection 
I cannot get my mind around what Thomas thought over the week\. It had to be on his mind\. He spent the last few years of his life with Jesus\. He saw miracles, he laughed and wept alongside the others\. His time with Jesus changed the essence of his life\. And then, he watched it come to a horrific end — Jesus was executed\.

No way this was a distant thought over those eight days\.

Today, it is common to say, “That experience was surreal\.” It refers to a real experience so bizarre that it seems unreal\. Could that event have happened?  Was it a dream? It was surreal\.

\(AI\) Thomas lived in a gap where others experienced a new reality, but he hadn’t\. Everything felt normal to him, yet people he trusted claimed something impossible had already happened\. That tension—between what is and what is said to be—created a disorienting, almost dreamlike state\. It seemed surreal\.

###### Personal
Waiting\.

No one likes to wait, especially in significant matters\. Thomas must have gone through many mental states as he tried to adjust to life without Jesus\. My imagination has him experiencing every emotion known to man\.

And Jesus kept him waiting\.

Strangely, this felt like the loving part\. At some level, Thomas had to believe and anticipate\. Maybe Jesus was letting the anticipation build\. Then, like the return of a loved one thought missing, Jesus appears\. Thomas believes\.

In my relationship with Jesus, I often wait\. 

Like Thomas, I too experience many emotions as I wait\. It is not a resting state for me\. \(Perhaps that lesson is the point\.\) When Jesus appears in my situations, I also move from doubt to belief\. A lifetime of the doubt\-belief pattern grows a depth of relation and deeper trust\.

Waiting in times of \(perceived\) urgency is never a passive emotion\. Yet, like Thomas, it would also not be correct to label me a “doubter\.”

TITLE: # The Raging Battle

# The Raging Battle
“For our struggle is not against flesh and blood, but against the rulers, against the authorities, against the powers of this world’s darkness, and against the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly realms\.”
‭‭— Ephesians‬ ‭6‬:‭12‬ ‭BSB‬‬

The struggle Christians face is often misunderstood\. When times are difficult, we call it a “battle,” and ask for prayer\. The battle is real, of course, but it can easily be turned in a badge of honor expressing who has it worst\. While the battle centers on use, it is not about us\. The battle is about Jesus\.

On the other end, the struggle Christians face is not understood enough\. The unseen battle is real, even when evidence of it seems like “normal” life\. We think, when our day goes well, the battle was calm, no need to think about it\. Living this way is woefully misinformed\. 

The apostle Paul spend almost three years in Ephesians teaching and caring for the church\. We have a few stories of spiritual encounters, yet not enough to capture “the day in the life of Paul\.” It might be easy to conclude that what is written is a comprehensive view of the spiritual battle, yet it is clear that much more occurred in Paul’s years in Ephesus\.

Paul gave instructions to the elders, sensing with certainty that he would never return to Ephesus\. He made a strong statement for the elders to care for themselves, one another, and others in the church\. A specific warning followed: 

“I know that fierce wolves will come in after I am gone, and they won’t spare the flock\. Yes, even from among yourselves people will arise, saying things which will distort the truth, and they will draw the disciples away after them\.”

A tale as old as time: attacks will come after I am gone, sparing no one\. The attack will be a frontal assault, beware, there will be well\-placed insiders that will also join in waging war\. No one is safe\.

The story line works because we know it well, and most have experienced various versions of the plot\.

N\.T\. Wright summarizes:  “The attack will then take the form, not of direct contradiction or a clash of powers, but of distorting the truth\. The greatest heresies do not come about by straightforward denial; most of the church will see that for what it is\. ​*They happen when an element which may even be important, but isn’t central, looms so large that people can’t help talking about it, fixating on it, debating different views of it as though this were the only thing that mattered*​\.’ \(Acts for Everyone\)

This is the history of Christian theological flash points: leadership authority, spiritual gifts, second coming, baptism, and on and on\. True, but even graver, Paul is suggesting something more destructive and personal\.

In a letter back to the church in Ephesus, Paul raises  the alarm again: “For our struggle is not against flesh and blood, but against *the rulers, against the authorities, against the powers of this world’s darkness, and against the spiritual forces of evil* in the heavenly realms\.” \(6‬:‭12\)
‬ ‭
A saying comes to mind: fighting in the dark with a hand tied behind your back\. The unseen is greater than the seen, and we struggle enough with what we can see\.

We are left sober minded\. Yet, amidst the stern warnings, Paul never suggests that we could loose the battle, only that we must fight it\. This gives great assurance, even bittersweet comfort, the victor has already taken the throne\.

TITLE: Urgency of the Last Word

Urgency of the Last Word
“What else is there to say? Just this: be strong in the Lord, and in the strength of his power\.”
‭‭—Ephesians‬ ‭6‬:‭10‬ ‭BFE

It makes sense in the life of the early church that it would be fragile and susceptible to incorrect teaching\. After all, nothing yet is able to take the place of the spoken word\.

Today, it is evident that, not only do we have the Bible in written form, we also have access to the greatest biblical scholars instantaneously at our fingertips\. \(Artificial intelligence has entered the public domain recently\.\) We conclude that we are not susceptible to the fragile issues the early church faced\.

Over the past decade, humanity has learned important lessons about how we misuse our flood of information\. The saying “with information comes great power” has taken on a new meaning\. The more information we have, the more opportunities there are to be misled\. Our daily awareness that information can be deceptive has caused us to become more mistrustful\. Our situation is much worse than what Paul could have imagined, yet the warning still remains relevant and important\. 

The battle that Paul was addressing remains very, very real\.

“What we have in the present passage, and what I believe is required again and again as Christians face the daily and yearly battle for the kingdom, is a sober, realistic assessment both of the struggle we are engaged in and of the weapons at our disposal\.” \(NT Wright\)

###### Perspective
Not only must Christian soldiers must step back and observe our surroundings, it is vital we also readily remind one another what the battle is about\. 

Deception is the weapon used most often, and we must not be deceived about our fight\. This battle is not about my growth as a Christian, nor about my well being, nor about “saving lost souls\.” At the core, our plight has little to do with the goal\. We are wagging war against a well\-integrated system that resists and rears down the rule of Jesus and the advancement of His Kingdom\.

***If one does not have an awareness of Christ’s kingdom around us, one will surely not see the fight raging against us\.***

This gives gravity to the admonition, “be strong in the Lord, and in the strength of his power\.” His power is what set Jesus upon the throne, never to be unseated, and that same power is source of expanding His kingdom\.

Paul will give us tools to wage war, but do not be deceived, even the strongest among us does not have enough power to overcome; that power only resides in the strength of Jesus\.

The perspective of every Christian is not personal victory by which we take the ground we determine important\. No\. Paul’s statements make it clear that our role is to employ the tools the Lord gave us to invoke His power\. The direction of where we use those tools lies in the hands of the Supreme General, for He is the only one that can see all the forces massed against us\.

In the truest sense, we are ALL soldiers, following one general\. While there is some logic to apply our levels\-of\-command, doing that is a kind to the blind\-leading\-the\-blade, forgetting major weapon we resist — deception\.
