# Proposal Narrative — Section 3: Program Design
## Proxima Partners | Lilly Endowment Exploring Christian Practices Initiative

---

### What We Have Already Learned

Before we describe the program, we want to share what the listening has taught us. The design that follows did not come from a planning retreat. It came from being in the room.

Early in our time in San Francisco, Mike met a man experiencing homelessness steps from his front door. He went to a friend who works with unsheltered people in the city and described his dilemma: he did not know whether to invite the man inside, feed him, or offer him a place to sleep. His friend started laughing and asked a simple question: "Do you even know his name?" That moment reframed everything. The goal was not the solution. The goal was the person. And the first step was not a leap to the top of the ladder. It was the next rung — learning a name, entering a story already in progress.

That reframe has shaped every conversation since. On Market Street, Mike stopped into a candy store with his dog, Remy. A young woman behind the counter lit up at the sight of the dog. The next week, Mike returned and asked about her dog. That question opened something. She had recently lost one of her dogs — an eight-pound service animal trained to detect her epileptic seizures. They talked longer than they should have, given that she was working. When Mike turned to leave, she called after him: "See you next week." She said it with a wide smile. Nobody planned that. Nobody presented the gospel. But something began.

A Christian friend — someone with good theology, genuine faith, and years of following Jesus — sat across from Mike in a long conversation and said something that has stayed with us. Nobody had ever given him a framework for watching God work in ordinary life. He did not know that was something you could do.

These are not rare moments. They are what we find every week in San Francisco. People who are spiritually curious but have no tradition to give their questions a form. Christians who love Jesus but have never been shown how to notice Him at work on a Tuesday. Neighbors, colleagues, and strangers who open up when someone simply pays attention. Our program design grows directly from what these conversations have revealed.

### Why These Practices, from This Tradition, in This City

San Francisco is a city that believes things are possible. Underneath the skepticism, there is something genuinely alive here: a hunger for meaning, for community, for lives that cohere around something true. But this city is also deeply skeptical of words. Every idea arrives pre-packaged with its counter-argument. Intellectual persuasion, on its own, rarely breaks through. What tends to reach people is a life that tells a story its words did not have to announce.

That observation is not a concession to the culture. It is a return to what Jesus described. On His final evening with the disciples, He gave one instruction for how the world would recognize them: love one another as I have loved you. He was not describing a communication technique. He was describing a way of being that carries its own credibility.

Our evangelical tradition has always understood this. The Pietists gathered in small groups because faith formed in community. The Wesleyans developed class meetings because accountability and honest conversation made the gospel real. The testimony tradition — naming God's presence in your own experience — has always been the practice that builds faith in the room even as it strengthens it in the speaker.

What we bring to San Francisco is the conviction that these practices work precisely because they do not begin with argument. They begin with presence, with honest questions, with the assumption that God is already at work in the person in front of you. Scripture is clear that the readiness of another person is God's work, not ours (Col. 4:3). An open door shows itself not through a formal invitation but through receptivity — pain, curiosity, moral honesty, genuine engagement. Our practices are designed to recognize those open doors and walk through them with integrity, never with manipulation.

This matters because San Francisco runs on leverage. People here chase influence with good intentions. But the power that actually changes lives does not sit still long enough to be seized. It shows up in the gap between what you planned and what actually happened. It looks, from the outside, a lot like weakness and a lot like trust. Our practices meet people in that gap.

### Key Activities

Proxima's program is organized around four interconnected activities. Each one delivers a Christian practice directly to participants. Together they form a complete arc from first contact to deepened formation to outward witness.

**Individual Discernment Coaching**

One-on-one coaching is the entry point for most people who engage Proxima. Sessions are structured around reflective questions, scripture, and prayer. They help individuals explore questions of calling, purpose, meaning, and faith in the specific circumstances of their own lives. Coaching is offered free of charge. It is open to anyone — Christian, unaffiliated, or anywhere in between — who is willing to ask honest questions about where God might be present in their life.

In Year 1 of the grant period, we will define and stabilize two coaching pathways: an Introductory Coaching track for individuals new to this kind of reflective practice, and an Unscripted Coaching track for individuals in ongoing formation who need space for the questions that do not fit a structured format. We will also develop a feedback loop — a consistent process for documenting what we learn from coaching conversations — that informs program refinement and contributes to the broader field of urban ministry practice. We project serving 25 individuals in coaching in Year 1, growing to 60 by Year 3 and 100 by Year 5 as additional coaching capacity is built.

**Cohort-Based Learning Communities**

Learning communities are small peer groups organized around the specific contexts where San Franciscans already gather and work. Rather than organizing by church membership or theological background, we organize by life context: technology and engineering, public education, neighborhoods, life stages, and professional networks. This design makes the groups accessible to spiritually curious individuals alongside committed Christians — and that mix is part of what makes them work.

Each learning community meets regularly over a sustained period. The content draws on Proxima's coaching framework, scripture, and the lived experience of participants. Participants practice faith-sharing, discernment, and testimony within the group — building the habits and language that carry into everyday relationships outside it.

In Year 1, we will pilot two learning communities in two distinct life contexts, developing a replicable facilitation model that can be trained and expanded. By Year 3, we anticipate running six active communities across four contexts, with trained facilitators beyond Katie and Mike leading some of them. By Year 5, we envision a network of twelve communities operating across San Francisco, with a documented and transferable model that other urban ministries can adopt.

**Testimony and Narrative Gatherings**

Periodically throughout the grant period, Proxima will convene larger gatherings — drawing from coaching participants, learning community members, and their broader relational networks — for structured testimony practice. These are not worship services. They are intentional opportunities for people at every stage of the spiritual journey to practice articulating their experience: what they are searching for, what they have found, what has changed. For the unaffiliated, these gatherings are often the first time they have been given language and a community for what they have been experiencing quietly on their own. For Christians, they build the capacity to share their faith naturally and without pressure in everyday life.

We plan to hold four testimony gatherings per year, growing in size and reach as the participant community expands.

**Insights and Resources**

Proxima documents and shares what it is learning. In Year 1 we will produce 15 micro-resources — short podcasts, video reflections, captured conversations, and written articles — drawn directly from the coaching and learning community work. These resources serve participants in between sessions. They also serve the broader network of Christians in San Francisco and beyond who are navigating the same questions. A beta version of Proxima Café — our online platform for curated insights and community conversation, currently in development — will be tested with learning community participants during Year 1.

### Where the Practices Live: Settings That Multiply Access

The people we seek to reach will not walk into a church to find these practices. The practices must live where they already are. Our listening has shown us exactly where that is.

**Coffee shops and neighborhood gathering places.** The longest and most honest conversations we have had in San Francisco happen over coffee. Not in offices. Not in church lobbies. At a table where someone feels at home. Places like Matching Half on McAllister, Tillage Workspace near the ballpark, and the small cafes along Divisadero are where trust is already being built and where real questions surface without prompting. Coaching sessions and learning community meetings happen in these settings because they lower every barrier the church inadvertently raises.

**Workplaces and professional networks.** San Francisco's identity is tied to its industries. When we organize learning communities by professional context — engineers, educators, healthcare workers — we meet people inside the world that shapes their daily questions. The engineer who wants to talk with a grieving colleague but has no language for it does not need a church program. He needs a community of people navigating the same tension, in his own professional world, with practices that help him act.

**Sidewalks, parks, and the rhythms of daily life.** Some of our most significant ministry moments have happened at Oracle Park during a baseball game, on a walk through the Panhandle, at a pancake breakfast in Golden Gate Park. Mike's dog, Remy, has opened more conversations than any outreach event could. A walk through the neighborhood without headphones, a greeting to a barista by name, a moment of prayer for a coworker instead of rushing past — these are not strategies. They are the daily settings where the practice of presence takes root. One coaching participant, Ana, began with these rhythms: walking her neighborhood, noticing God's activity in her daily routines. As she grew more present to others, neighbors opened up and friendships deepened. She eventually invited a friend to practice the same habits together. That is multiplication through daily life.

**Church partners as ecclesial anchors.** Our 17 church partners provide community, accountability, and connection to the broader life of the church for participants who are ready for it. Key partners include Christ Church SF, Citizens Church, Resonate Church, and Solano Community Church, along with the EFCA West District. But the primary landscape is the city itself — not because we are indifferent to the church, but because the people we seek to reach are not yet inside it. We go to them first. The practices make the path.
